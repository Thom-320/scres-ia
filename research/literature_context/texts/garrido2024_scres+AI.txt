       Abstract. The increasing complexity of supply chain (SC) networks and the asso-
       ciated risks have captured global attention, leading to the emergence of the con-
       cept of supply chain resilience (SCRES). Over the past two decades, SCRES has
       been a focal point of research, explored through various perspectives, approaches,
       and tools. Among these, the discrete event simulation (DES) technique stands
       out for its effectiveness in modeling SCRES. While DES models offer multiple
       advantages and have been widely used in the literature, they lack the capability
       to measure a crucial element of SCRES: the cumulative learning of a SC net-
       work as it experiences risk events over time. The absence of this attribute renders
       attempts to operationalize SCRES incomplete. This research aims to address this
       methodological gap by proposing–from a theoretical standpoint–the integration
       of artificial intelligence (AI) algorithms into DES models. The research delves
       into several categories of AI algorithms that can learn from successive iterations
       of DES models. Based on this exploratory analysis, it is suggested that neural
       networks, particularly backpropagation, Kolmogorov-Arnold, and reinforcement
       learning algorithms, are the most suitable to address this gap in the literature. Addi-
       tionally, a novel definition of SCRES is proposed, emphasizing the importance of
       learning within supply chain networks.

       Keywords: Supply chain resilience · SCRES · Artificial intelligence · Machine
       learning · Simulation models · Neural networks



1 Introduction
A significant feature that characterizes globally operating commercial industries lies in
their strong emphasis on continuous learning to enhance its manufacturing processes
and technical procedures [15]. For instance, in the aviation industry, whenever a major
or minor incident occurs during flight operations, valuable lessons are garnered and
disseminated across all the nodes of the aircraft supply chain (SC), though with some
exceptions [11]. This practice stems from the industry’s high-stakes nature, where errors

can have serious or even fatal consequences. The progressive accumulation of knowl-
edge and experiences converge into new updated policies, recommendations, and best
practices, all aimed at preventing or minimizing the impact of similar future risky events.
As a result, each new aircraft model launched to market and their operational methods,
are typically safer and more reliable [57]. This approach of learning from past incidents
is not unique to the commercial aviation industry. In practice, many other business orga-
nizations have adopted a similar mindset, making them more resilient to unexpected
disruptions [20].
    A classic definition of organizational learning–provided by Levitt and March [31]–
describes this concept from three essential elements: routines, history-dependent, and
target-oriented. In other words, SC networks engage in learning by extracting insights
from their past experiences, which are subsequently transformed into established routines
that shape and guide their future behavior. In parallel, the paradigm of supply chain
resilience or SCRES–understood as the adaptive capacity of supply networks to endure
unforeseen disruptions and recover to a desired state [23]–has tacitly integrated the notion
of learning since the beginning [13, 42, 48]. Nonetheless, the literature on SCRES has
overlooked this crucial managerial capability by encapsulating it through other related
concepts such as adaptability, viability, or flexibility [43, 49, 53]. This oversight has
been particularly prominent in research works focusing on the operationalizing of the
notion of SCRES [6, 8].
    The current article hypothesizes the existence of a methodological gap in the literature
upon the operationalization of the SCRES concept, primarily due to the complexity
of incorporating supply chain learning (SCL) attributes into resilience models. This
proposal highlights the need for more comprehensive frameworks that can effectively
integrate these learning dynamics to improve the credibility and validity of SCRES
metrics available in the literature. This gap may be further exacerbated by the lack
of explicit references to SCL in SCRES conceptualizations [19]. Equally important,
by addressing this gap, future investigations could enhance their credibility of SCRES
metrics, leading to more precise and realistic metrics of SCRES. To this end, this study
explores the most suitable artificial intelligence (AI) algorithms that can be integrated
in SCRES models. We are only interested in SCRES models based on the utilization of
discrete-event simulation (DES). In this sense, it is important to note that the purpose of
the current research should not be confused with studies that examine the advantages of
implementing AI algorithms to enhance SCRES, or to assist SC networks in navigating
today’s volatile and uncertain environments [56]. In fact–due to space limitations–, the
scope of this study is purely exploratory; however, it lays the groundwork for future,
more in-depth research.
    With this in mind, the following guiding questions are formulated: (1) What category
of AI algorithms best mimic the SCL attribute and, (2) how this AI algorithm family can
be integrated in the internal structure of a DES model for SCRES assessment? Regarding
these questions, various studies have emphasized the need to couple AI algorithms with
simulation models. For instance, in a study upon the relationship between medium-
term decisions in production planning and demand variability, Garrido et al., [24] posed
the idea of combining robust, learning-based algorithms to improve factory resilience
through simulation models. Zhang et al. [56] suggested that the use of predictive analytics

models along with simulation approaches, can effectively describe the dynamic behavior
of SC under conditions of uncertainty. Von Rueden et al. [54] pointed out that the use of
simulation models can enrich unsupervised machine learning (ML) algorithms. Greasley
and Edwards [25] indicated that DES models can generate synthetic data for training
and testing ML algorithms. Additionally, these authors pointed out that DES models can
benefit from the capability of ML techniques to capture and simplify complex processes
for the model. Moreover, da Silva et al. [16] argued that the use of simulation techniques
combined with ML algorithms can help to verify the accuracy of the models used from
a dynamic system perspective. Thus, from a theoretical point of view, addressing this
methodological gap will allow to expand the impact’s understanding of the SCL attribute
in SCRES metrics, by considering resilience as a dynamic adaptive capability rather than
a static one [9]. That is, coupling AI algorithms with SCRES-based simulation models
may correct the characteristic ‘Alzheimer effect’ observed in this type of studies, in
which the modeled SC network fails to retain information from previous experiences–or
simulation scenarios.
    The structure of this research is as follows: Sect. 2 examines the most relevant
previous works on SCRES using DES models, highlighting their advantages and main
limitations. Section 3 provides a detailed explanation of the steps required to build a
DES model for SCRES assessment. Section 4 presents a comprehensive roadmap of
AI algorithms based on Powell’s classification [38], explores the integration of neural
network algorithms with DES models for SCRES assessment, and introduces a new
definition of SCRES based on the notion of Supply Chain Learning (SCL). Finally,
Sect. 5 presents the main conclusions of this exploratory analysis and offers suggestions
for future research in the SCRES area.


2 Previous Works on SCRES-Based Simulation Models

The existing literature for measurement SCRES falls into two main areas: qualitative
and quantitative approaches [35]. The first type of studies mainly comprises the use of
surveys, expert opinion panels, cases of study, and empirical methods [37, 41]. On the
other hand, SCRES-based quantitative studies employ a varied amalgam of methodolo-
gies, grounded on the utilization of mathematical/analytical models such as optimiza-
tion/metaheuristics programming [21], and simulation [51]. Among these two main
areas, a qualitative approach seems to be the most prevalent method for SCRES analysis
[32]. However, despite many of its well-known advantages, qualitative approaches have
been severely criticized due to problems with generalizability and transferability of the
results derived [23]. As mentioned earlier, this research focuses only on the quantita-
tive approach. Similarly to previous analysis, quantitative approaches have also been the
subject of criticism in the literature [7]. The most incisive appraisal is their inherent math-
ematical complexity, which often makes these models inaccessible or even inapplicable
to many real SC scenarios [38].
             Enhancing the Operationalization of SCRES-Based Simulation Models         83

     Of all the models that make up SCRES-based quantitative approach, the current
research will focus only on simulation-based studies. The choice of simulation models
stems from their ability to dynamically analyze SC networks under risk conditions [30].
This relevant characteristic in SC offers a significant leverage over other quantitative
methodologies for evaluating SCRES, especially when integrated with AI algorithms.
Thus, depending on the specific research objective set, simulation studies for measuring
SCRES involve the use of various techniques, including Monte Carlo simulation, agent-
based simulation, system dynamics, hybrid simulation, and DES models. According to
Garrido [23] and Saisridhar et al. [44], of the five mentioned simulation techniques, the
latter model has been consistently the most extended method for measuring SCRES in
literature. Researchers appear to favor DES models due to four primary reasons. First,
DES models offer ease and speed to generate large volumes of synthetic data for SC
networks at very low cost [10]; second, DES models excel in capturing various types
of SC disruptions and their effects, including transient and rare stochastic events [56];
third, the utilization of DES models allows to represent SC networks as complex systems,
based on the flow of materials and information [36]; and lastly, DES models have been
widely utilized across various SC scenarios to assess SCRES [28, 33, 34]. In this sense,
it is worth noting that DES models facilitate the relatively straightforward recreation
of various risk mitigation strategies within SC [17]. This capability enables researchers
and practitioners to systematically evaluate the effectiveness of different approaches,
thereby enhancing the resilience and efficiency of SC operations [50].
     Summarizing the above, although the prior literature review is not comprehensive
in nature, it adequately represents the state of the art in SCRES-based DES models.
Notably, in all of these studies, the SCL attribute is an absent element that needs to
be properly incorporated to enhance the credibility and validity of SCRES metrics.
The following section outlines the steps to construct a SCRES metric from a DES
model, while also explaining why this type of SC approach does not incorporate the
SCL attribute.


3 Archetype of a DES-Based Model for SCRES Measurement
In general, the process of building a DES-based model for SCRES measurement encom-
passes several stages. Figure 1 illustrates the set of steps required to this end. Step 1
involves the selection of the SC network under consideration. Ideally, the DES model
should be based on data from a real SC network. Nevertheless, if the SC information
needed is unavailable or incomplete, a hypothetical SC may be used as substitute–that
is, build a model based on information assumptions. Step 2 involves the formulation of
a guiding question and/or a set of research hypotheses that will guide the development
of the DES model. Given that the primary objective of the current analysis is to enhance
the operationalization of the SCRES, much of the theory-driven research in this area is
based on conceptual blueprints describing moderating effects [1, 3]. This type of the-
oretical models contains postulates of the form ‘how variable x relates to variable y in
presence of variable z’, with the SCRES metric represented by the y or z variable. Step
3 consists of gathering essential data of the selected SC network in step 1, particularly,
from the material and information flows. This information is usually found in flow-charts
84        A. Garrido et al.

and value stream maps. In Step 4, the potential risk events that have impacted or could
directly affect the performance of the SC under analysis are identified and documented
in detail. In this respect, it can be said that a SC can be affected by three types of
risk events: inherent risks–or known-knows–, natural disasters and terrorist attacks–or
known-unknows–, and black swan events–or unknown-unknows [23, 52]. Step 5 defines
and implements assumptions about key aspects of the DES model. This process typi-
cally involves imposing simplifying conditions on the material and information flows
of the SC under analysis. Step 6 consists in determining both the potential impact of
each SC risk previously considered, and then to assess their probability of occurrence.
This prospective analysis operates on the premise that the prior identified risk events in
step 4 will effectively materialize in the future. In Step 7, the initial pilot version of the
DES model is executed and proved. If everything is as expected, in Step 8, the output
results of the DES model undergo statistical verification and validation. In this way, in
the verification phase, the model results are analyzed to ensure that they fall within the
expected acceptability ranges. If the DES model passes this verification, it proceeds to
the validation phase, where the output values are statistically compared to the actual SC
data, by using regularly a non-parametric statistical test [55]. In Step 9, a comprehensive
simulation experimental design should be developed and followed. In this analysis, the
number of the SC configurations–or variations–and independent replications are set. The
experimental simulation design serves to statistically evaluate the research hypotheses
posited in step 2. The previous three procedures–verification, validation, and simulation
experimental design–are key for increasing the credibility of the output results of the
DES model. In Step 10, the warm-up period and total duration for the simulation exper-
iment are both determined. These two criteria are essential to prevent collecting flawed
simulation data before the DES model has reached its steady-state condition [4]. This
procedure is necessary to gather sufficient data for the subsequent statistical analysis [2].
In Step 11, a quantitative metric for SCRES suitable to be integrated into the DES model
is developed. This SCRES metric should be constructed based on the output variables
of the DES model. Lastly, in Step 12, the main findings from the DES model are docu-
mented and presented to the stakeholders. During this final step, the guiding question is
either validated or refuted, depending on the outcomes of the DES model.
    The Fig. 2 summarizes the implementation sequence for the previous simulation
procedure in a SC network over multiple iterations or runs. Notably, this diagram shows
the integration of the decision variables, the output variables, and the metric of SCRES.
Moreover, Fig. 2 also highlights the function of organizational learning L, revealing the
simulation SC network’s inability to learn from risk events after multiple iterations. In
this research, we named this phenomenon as the Alzheimer’s effect in simulation models,
drawing parallels to the medical condition observed in humans. So, why the SC network
shown in Fig. 2 is unable to learn? Although this question will be addressed in detail in
the next section, an observant reader may have noticed that steps ➌ and ➑ in Fig. 2 were
intentionally highlighted. This is because in DES models for SCRES assessment, both
steps represent the two extremes of an open-loop SC network. To build a DES model
that can learn from the occurrence of past events and eventually increase SCRES, an AI
algorithm must be integrated between these two nodes, as a way of bridge to convert

the open-loop SC network into a closed-loop system. In the following section, we will
thoroughly examine various categories of AI algorithms capable of performing this task.

4 Integration of AI Algorithms to Emulate Organizational
  Learning in SCRES-Based DES Models

4.1 Typology of AI Algorithms
The field of AI algorithms is vast and complex, involving the utilization of sophisticated
statistical methods, computational architecture, and advanced mathematical techniques.
AI represents a broad range of technologies that can integrate huge amounts of data,
sophisticated algorithms, and substantial computing power to carry out tasks typically
requiring human intellect [5]. In this regard, although several classifications have been
provided [29], the boundaries between the different AI types/categories remain unclear
and often overlapped. Figure 3 provides a general framework for the different types of
AI, based on Powell’s classification [39]. Following this author, the field of AI can be
divided into two main areas: simple behaviors and machine learning. In Fig. 3, the first
category comprises AI algorithms based on the use of logic rules. Powell states that this
subset includes rule engines dedicated to routine tasks. A practical example of this type
              Enhancing the Operationalization of SCRES-Based Simulation Models           87

of AI algorithm is a chatbot, which is usually used in the logistics field for order tracking
and updates.
    The second principal category in Fig. 3 comprises three distinctive levels of AI algo-
rithms: basic machine learning (ML), pattern recognition, and large language models
(LLM). Basic-ML is a family of general-purpose AI algorithms. This type of algorithms
shapes the landscape of business analytics and are known by their notable adaptability
to diverse scenarios. Basic-ML algorithms can be employed to uncover compelling pat-
terns from structured datasets–numerical, textual, or a combination thereof–, primarily
sourced from ERP/CRM systems, websites, and/or social media platforms [14]. Typical
examples of basic-ML algorithms are the general linear models or GLMs. GLMs encom-
pass several conventional linear regression models for a continuous response variable,
given continuous and/or categorical predictors. These include multiple linear regres-
sion, logistic/Gaussian regression, and ANOVA/ANCOVA models with fixed effects
only, among others.
    The subsequent level in Fig. 3 comprises AI algorithms specialized in recognizing
diverse patterns. The most emblematic AI algorithms in this category are neural networks
(NNs). These robust algorithms are named for their structural and functional resemblance
to biological neural networks, as well as their information processing capabilities [22]. A
key feature of NNs relies on their capacity to learn from diversified datasets. However, the
learning process in NNs typically involves complex training sequences and vast amounts
of data, making it costly in terms of both time and financial resources. Since the mid-
1990s, NNs have been employed to recognize several types of patterns, including images,
voices, stock market predictions, text generation, language translation, fraud prevention,
autonomous driving, genetic analysis, disease diagnosis, and even have been used to
classify coconuts [18]. Within the field of NNs, algorithms specialized in processing
and extracting complex non-linear patterns from non-numerical datasets, such as voice
and images, are known as deep neural networks (DNNs). DNNs are the most widely used
AI models by practitioners for image and voice classification [45]. According to Chakir
et al. [12], DNNs mirror the functions of the human brain–particularly the temporal
lobes–and can include hundreds of connections and layers.
    Lastly, at the uppermost level of Fig. 3, we find large language models (LLMs)–
aka generative AI algorithms–, which represent the most advanced technology currently
available. This revolutionary category of AI algorithms enables the generation of diverse
content forms, including images, music, written text, and intricate outputs like virtual
environments [27]. Generative AI algorithms–similarly to NNs–, operate by training on
large datasets to produce new models that mimic the patterns and structures of the input
data [40]. It is not an exaggeration to state that LLMs significantly enhance computational
intelligence, potentially surpassing human capabilities.

4.2 Coupling AI Algorithms with DES Models for SCRES Assessment

Next we return to the question of interest, which of the four levels of AI algorithms shown
in Fig. 3 best mimics the learning process of a SC network shaped by a DES model?
This is undoubtedly a challenging endeavor, as to the best of the authors’ knowledge,
no previous research has addressed the methodological gap of SCL in DES models for
SCRES assessment using AI algorithms. In order to provide a plausible answer to this
question, based on Garrido [23]’s work, Figs. 4a–d describe the archetype drivers of a
DES model built to evaluate SCRES. In the example illustrated in Fig. 4, the Garrido
[23]’s analysis selected four specific drivers for the SCRES metric–autotomy period,
recovery period, disruption period, and fill rate–, though any other set of drivers could
have been chosen. Furthermore, Fig. 4e denotes a metric of SCRES (ReT ). Several
interesting features can be derived from the whole analysis shown in Fig. 4. First, to
avoid scalability problems, most quantitative research approaches to operationalizing
SCRES have opted to normalize the output range between 0 and 100 [23, 26, 47]. The
normalization of the SCRES metric greatly simplifies its scrutiny, making it easier to
understand and interpret, as well as facilitating comparison with other similar studies.
Second, the SCRES metric shown in Fig. 4e typically determines the output variable for
a DES model by integrating independent and continuous variables (drivers). However,
depending on the nature of the problem under examination, this metric can also be
expressed as a numerical binary output variable [46]. And third, for the research analysis
described in Fig. 4, inventory buffers and manufacturing capacity were the two decision

    Therefore, by contrasting the drivers and metric of SCRES in Fig. 4 with the four
levels of AI algorithms depicted in Fig. 3, we can preliminarily conclude that level 3–or
pattern recognition–best fits the methodological research problem raised in Fig. 2. This
deduction is based on two main premises. Firstly, the methodological problem of SCL in
DES models for SCRES assessment is intrinsically related to pattern recognition mecha-
nisms. While DES techniques have conclusively demonstrated their efficacy in modeling
SC networks under uncertainty, they fall short in incorporating lessons learned from suc-
cessive simulations runs. In practice, when simulating a SC network, the engineers must
design an efficient–though costly–experimental framework. From the collected data,
they must then identify–or learn–the configuration that optimally enhances the SCRES
metric. This iterative procedure is both inefficient and imprecise. And secondly, as indi-
cated in Fig. 3, the quintessence of level 3 are NNs. As mentioned earlier, NNs allow
completing nearly any learning activity, including activities of classification, numeric
prediction, and unsupervised pattern recognition. All these tasks are closely related to
the methodological challenge posed in Fig. 2.

     The directed network diagram of Fig. 5 shows how possibly a NN model might be
used to join the two disconnected nodes highlighted in Fig. 2. Thus, in Fig. 5, the rela-
tionship between the input signals received by the SCRES drivers di –or dendrites–and
the output axon–or SCRES metric–is made explicit. Drawing an analogy to a biological
neuron, each SCRES driver signal di is weighted based on the ρ values, according to
its importance, using a mathematical algorithm or AI approach. The SCRES drivers di
are summed by the cell body, and the signal is then processed according to an activation
function f . An example of an activation function f could be, ‘Is the SCRES measure at
configuration x higher than in the previous configuration (x–1)?’ Thus, the artificial neu-
ron depicted in Fig. 5 could mimic the organizational learning L and be integrated with
a DES model for SCRES assessment. This improvement can be considered a method-
ological game-changer for future investigations in the operationalization of SCRES. As
a result of the previous analysis, we are compelled to provide a new definition of SCRES
integrating the notion of SCL:
     SCRES is the progressive adaptive capacity of a SC network, derived from continuous
learning based on past experiences. This learning enables a SC to respond effectively to
disturbances caused by known-known, known-unknown, and unknown-unknown events.
Furthermore, it allows a SC network to recover from these disruptions and return to its
original or a desired state.

5 Conclusions

It is a fact that DES models have struggled to effectively integrate organizational learning
into measures of SCRES. Consequently, metrics for SCRES-based DES models may be
deemed inadequate or incomplete. Additionally, despite the growing number of publi-
cations on operationalization of SCRES and the salient methodological gap indicated
               Enhancing the Operationalization of SCRES-Based Simulation Models                   91

in present research, relatively few studies have explored combined solutions integrat-
ing AI algorithms into DES models for SCRES measurement. We coined this problem
‘the Alzheimer’s effect in SCs’, referring the taken assumption of the SC network in
forgetting the learnt from past events. The analysis developed in this article provides
an initial exploration aimed at finding solutions to this methodological challenge. As
a main conclusion, we foresee that if AI algorithms are integrated with DES models
for measuring SCRES, the validity and credibility of this approach will dramatically
increase. However, regarding the two posited questions, it is necessary to underline that
the scope of this research is purely exploratory in nature. This means that this research
should be considered just as the initial step for future formal and deeper investigations in
the domain of SCRES measurement models. We demonstrated that the analysis derived
from DES models for SCRES measurement is not only a time-consuming iterative pro-
cess, but also prone to yield large systematic errors. In other words, it is a problem
type where humans deal with a well-structured problem where machines can outper-
form humans, pointing out to an inefficient process. In this sense we envision three
possible specific alternatives to the problem raised: back propagation neural networks,
Kolmogorov-Arnold neural networks, and simulation-optimization approach as a form
of reinforcement learning. Each of these AI approaches has the potential to effectively
solve the problem considered.
